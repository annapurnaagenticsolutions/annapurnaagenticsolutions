The Stories We Tell Our Children Shape the Technology They Build

Annapurna Agentic Solutions · Archive

Theme: The Philosophy

Length: ~700 words

Hook: Cultural — connect heritage to tech values

We're building a storybook platform for Indian children. It has stories from the Bhagavad Gita, the Ramayana, the lives of Indian saints, and the folklore of Odisha.

We're also building AI governance tools for enterprises.

These might seem unrelated. They're not.

The stories we tell our children become the values they carry into adulthood. The values they carry into adulthood become the technology they build.

If we want ethical AI, we need ethical humans. If we want ethical humans, we need to start with the stories we tell them when they're young.

The problem with removing stories from education.

India's education system has been steadily degrading its cultural content. History is reduced to dates. Literature is reduced to exam passages. Stories are reduced to "moral science" classes that nobody takes seriously.

Meanwhile, the stories children DO consume come from YouTube, Hollywood, and social media. The values they absorb are: consumption, individualism, and instant gratification.

We're not against Western stories. We're against ONLY Western stories. A child who grows up watching Marvel but has never heard of Arjuna's doubt on the battlefield is a child with an incomplete moral vocabulary.

What stories teach that STEM can't.

The Bhagavad Gita teaches duty without attachment to results. The Ramayana teaches integrity under adversity. The lives of Indian saints teach devotion across caste, gender, and class boundaries. Odisha folklore teaches that wisdom comes from unexpected places.

These aren't religious lessons. They're human lessons. They're the foundation of ethical reasoning:

  • Duty without attachment → "I will build this AI system because it's the right thing to do, not because it will make me rich."
  • Integrity under adversity → "I will flag this bias even though my manager wants to ship."
  • Devotion across boundaries → "This AI should work for everyone, not just people who look like me."
  • Wisdom from unexpected places → "The best insight on this problem might come from the user, not the engineer."

    • What we're building.

    HeritageWorld — a world within Jigyasu that brings Indian stories to children in their own language, with interactive storybooks, illustrated narratives, and age-appropriate retellings.

    The stories aren't preachy. They're not "moral science." They're stories — with heroes, conflicts, dilemmas, and resolutions. The values are embedded, not lectured.

    The connection to AI governance.

    When we build AgentOps Mesh — our open-source governance framework for AI agents — the core principles are:

    1. Transparency: Every agent action is visible. No black boxes.
    2. Accountability: Every agent has a human sponsor.
    3. Predictability: Agent behavior is bounded and tested.
    4. Equity: Policies prevent discriminatory outcomes.

      5. Where did these principles come from? Not from a framework. Not from a whitepaper. From values we learned from stories:

      • Transparency comes from the Indian tradition of open debate — from Shankara's digvijaya to Gandhi's public salt march.
      • Accountability comes from the Gita's insistence on duty — you act, and you own your actions.
      • Predictability comes from the Indian legal tradition — from Kautilya's Arthashastra to the Constitution.
      • Equity comes from the saints — from Kabir's "no caste in the eyes of God" to Ambedkar's "equality of opportunity."

        • The point.

        We're not saying Indian stories are better than other stories. We're saying that stories matter. That the narratives we give children shape the adults they become. And that the adults they become shape the technology they build.

        If we want technology that serves humanity, we need technologists who understand humanity. And that understanding starts with stories.

        We're building HeritageWorld because a child who knows Arjuna's dilemma will, twenty years from now, be the engineer who pauses before deploying an AI system and asks: "Is this the right thing to do?"

        That question is worth more than any framework we could build.
