PRAMANA · DPDP GLOSSARY

Key terms, in plain language.

The DPDP Act introduces a precise vocabulary. Using any of these terms loosely — or confusing them with similar terms from GDPR or other frameworks — is one of the most common compliance errors. These definitions are written for plain-language clarity and cross-checked against the DPDP Act 2023 primary text.

Certain Legitimate Uses (Section 7)

A closed, specific list of situations where personal data can be processed without consent — employment necessity, medical emergencies, state functions, and data voluntarily made public by the individual.

Unlike GDPR’s open-ended “legitimate interest” test, this list cannot be expanded by a business’s own judgement. If a use-case is not on this specific list, consent is required. There is no room for interpretation.

How Pramana uses this: any AI action that invokes a “legitimate use” claim is checked against this closed list before it proceeds. A claim that doesn’t match one of the seven listed categories is held for review.

Consent Manager (Section 2(g))

A specific, government-registered intermediary that lets individuals manage and withdraw consent across multiple companies through one interface. Registration requires meeting minimum financial and operational criteria; records must be maintained for a defined retention period.

A Consent Manager is a regulated entity with its own registration obligations — not a generic description of how a company manages consent internally.

⚠️ Note: Pramana governs consent enforcement within your own systems. It is not a registered Consent Manager under Section 2(g), and does not act as one on your behalf.

Cross-Border Data Transfer

Sending personal data outside India’s territory. DPDP’s approach is the opposite of GDPR: transfers are permitted to any country by default, except where the government explicitly restricts a specific destination.

This negative-list model means Indian businesses should not apply GDPR adequacy-decision logic here. The question is: is the destination on the government’s restricted list? If not, the transfer is generally permitted under DPDP (though sectoral rules may still apply).

Data Fiduciary (Section 2(i))

The entity — company, individual, or organisation — that decides the purpose and means of processing personal data. If your business collects a customer’s name, phone number, email address, health records, or purchase history, you are a Data Fiduciary under DPDP.

The term “fiduciary” is deliberate: the law places you in a position of trust with respect to the data you hold. The obligations that follow — notice, consent, security, rights fulfilment — flow from that trust relationship.

Data Principal (Section 2(j))

The individual whose personal data is being processed. DPDP’s term for what GDPR calls a “data subject.”

Your customers, users, patients, students, employees — anyone whose personal information you hold or process — are Data Principals. They have four statutory rights under DPDP: access, correction and erasure, grievance redressal, and the right to nominate.

Data Processor (Section 2(k))

An entity that processes personal data on behalf of a Data Fiduciary, under the Data Fiduciary’s instructions. Your cloud vendor, your analytics provider, your customer support software company — any third party handling your customers’ data on your behalf may be a Data Processor.

As a Data Fiduciary, you remain responsible for how your Data Processors handle the data you share with them. Written agreements with Data Processors are a baseline DPDP expectation.

Data Protection Board of India (DPBI) (Section 18)

The independent statutory body established under DPDP to receive complaints, investigate businesses, hold adjudicatory proceedings, and impose financial penalties. It operates with the powers of a civil court for purposes of evidence and proceedings.

The DPBI is already operational — complaints from the public are being accepted today. Full financial penalties become enforceable on 13 May 2027. Appeals from DPBI decisions go to the Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal (TDSAT).

Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA)

A structured assessment of how a data-processing activity affects individuals’ rights and safety. Required periodically for Significant Data Fiduciaries. A DPIA identifies risks before they become incidents, and documents that the organisation considered those risks.

Pramana’s evidence ledger provides the governance decision history that a DPIA review typically requires as inputs — what decisions were made about what data, under what rules, and with what outcomes.

Data Protection Officer (DPO)

The designated individual responsible for overseeing data-protection compliance and handling grievances from Data Principals. For Significant Data Fiduciaries, the DPO must be based in India and report to the organisation’s board-level governance.

For other organisations, a Grievance Officer plays a similar function — a named, accessible contact for data-related complaints.

Grievance Officer

The nominated contact — or the DPO, for Significant Data Fiduciaries — responsible for receiving and responding to Data Principals’ complaints about how their data has been handled. Contact details must be published.

Grievances that are not resolved within the required window can be escalated directly to the DPBI. The response timeline and all communications should be documented, since the DPBI may request them in any subsequent investigation.

Notice (Section 5)

The mandatory disclosure a Data Fiduciary must give before or at the point of collecting consent. It must explain: what personal data is being collected, the purpose, how Data Principals can exercise their rights, and how they can reach the Grievance Officer.

The notice must be in the language in which consent is actually sought — not only in English. DPDP Rules 2025 explicitly address multilingual notice requirements.

Personal Data Breach

Unauthorised processing, or accidental disclosure, loss, destruction, or alteration of personal data. Any confirmed breach triggers notification obligations: the Data Protection Board and affected individuals must be notified promptly.

The content, scope, and timing of breach notifications are legal decisions that must be made deliberately by authorised individuals — not automatically generated and sent by an AI system.

Right to Nominate (Section 14)

A Data Principal’s right to designate another individual to exercise their data rights — access, correction, erasure, grievance — in the event of their death or incapacity.

This provision is distinctive to India’s DPDP framework. It has no direct equivalent in GDPR or most other global privacy laws. Organisations need a process to verify a nominee’s authority and honour the nomination — treating it as an edge case to figure out when it happens is not sufficient.

Significant Data Fiduciary (SDF) (Section 10)

A Data Fiduciary the government designates for heightened obligations, based on the volume, sensitivity, and risk profile of the personal data it processes and the potential harm to individuals. SDF designation is made by the government — it is not self-declared.

Designated SDFs carry additional requirements: an India-based DPO reporting to the board, mandatory independent data audits, and periodic Data Protection Impact Assessments. Organisations in banking, healthcare, and large-scale digital platforms are likely candidates.
