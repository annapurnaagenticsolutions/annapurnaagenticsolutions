BEFORE WE SHOW YOU THE PRODUCT

Three things every Indian business using AI needs to understand.

Not technical concepts. Business realities — that are already in force.

01 · THE LAW — DPDP ACT 2023

India passed a new law about your customers’ data.

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 says: if your business stores or uses anyone’s personal information digitally — their name, phone number, health records, financial details, purchase history — you must protect it, use it only for what they agreed to, and be able to prove you did.

This applies to every business in India that has a customer database. Not just large corporations. A clinic with patient records. A coaching centre with student details. An NBFC with loan applications. An online store with delivery addresses. All of them.

💡 Think of it like the Food Safety Act — but for personal information. The law doesn’t tell you what business to run. It says how safely you must handle your customers’ data — and you need to prove it if someone questions you.

02 · THE REGULATOR — DPBI

The government created an official board to investigate and fine you.

The Data Protection Board of India (DPBI) is an independent government body — think of it like SEBI, but for personal data instead of financial markets. It accepts complaints directly from the public, investigates businesses, holds formal hearings like a court, and can issue large financial fines.

It is already operational. Any of your customers can file a complaint with the DPBI today. Full financial penalties begin on 13 May 2027. The complaints filed before that date will be adjudicated exactly when the fines become enforceable.

⚠️ This is not a guideline or an advisory. It is an active regulatory body with the legal authority of a court and fines that can reach ₹250 crore for a single violation category.

03 · THE PROBLEM — YOUR AI

AI makes this harder — because it moves faster than you can watch.

While you are in a meeting, your AI system may be making hundreds of decisions about your customers’ data. It might share information the customer never agreed to share. It might cite a rule that does not exist in the law. It might deny someone a loan, a service, or an opportunity — based on criteria that violate DPDP.

You will not know until a customer complains to the DPBI. And when the Board asks: “Show us what rule your AI followed when it made this decision” — you need an answer ready. Not a reconstruction. Not a guess. A proof.

💼 Imagine hiring an assistant who works 24 hours a day, making thousands of decisions without asking you. You are still legally responsible for every decision they made. AI is that assistant — and it works a lot faster.

THE LAW IN PRACTICE

Five real situations. What happened. What the law says.

These are not hypotheticals. These are the kinds of decisions AI systems make every day — in businesses like yours. Here is what Pramana does about each one, and why the law cares.

HELD FOR REVIEW

Your AI cited a law that does not exist.

What happened: A customer asked your AI chatbot about their data rights. The AI replied: “Under Section 47 of the DPDP Act, we can hold your data for five years.” Section 47 does not exist in the DPDP Act. The AI invented it.

What Pramana did: Caught the error before it reached the customer. Held the response for a human to review and correct. The customer saw nothing. No false legal claim went out in your company’s name.

⚖️ Why the law cares: Telling a customer something false about their legal rights — even through an AI — is your company’s legal statement. If the customer relied on that incorrect advice and later complained, you would have no defence. AI hallucinating law is not a technical curiosity — it is a legal liability.

BLOCKED

Your AI shared a customer’s data without their permission.

What happened: A customer called your helpline about a billing problem. Your AI, trying to be helpful, automatically shared their contact details with your marketing team to send a promotional offer. But the customer never agreed to that — they gave their number for customer support, not marketing.

What Pramana did: Blocked the data transfer before it happened. The marketing team never received it. The attempt was logged — with a timestamp, the rule that blocked it, and the reason.

⚖️ Why the law cares: DPDP requires that data collected for one purpose cannot be used for another without fresh consent. Using customer support data for marketing is a violation. Fine: up to ₹50 crore. The block receipt is your proof the safeguard was working.

HUMAN REVIEW

Your AI automatically rejected a loan application.

What happened: An AI system recommended rejecting a loan application. The system was about to send the rejection automatically — no human had reviewed it. The decision would have affected this person’s financial life, made entirely by a machine.

What Pramana did: Stopped the rejection from being sent. Required a human to review and approve the decision. The reviewer’s identity and the time of their approval were sealed in the record alongside the AI’s recommendation.

⚖️ Why the law cares: Significant automated decisions that affect individuals — financial access, service denial, employment — must be reviewable by a human. A machine-only decision with no human in the loop and no documented record is exactly what DPBI will ask about first.

BLOCKED

Your AI tried to use a child’s data without parental consent.

What happened: A student’s information was in your system. An AI scheduled a marketing message to be sent to the student’s profile — without checking whether parental consent was on file. The student was under 18.

What Pramana did: Checked whether verified parental consent existed before the message was sent. It did not. The action was blocked. The student’s record was unchanged. The block was logged.

⚖️ Why the law cares: Processing data about anyone under 18 without verifiable parental consent is one of the most serious violations under DPDP. Fine: up to ₹200 crore. The refusal to proceed — and the sealed receipt of that refusal — is evidence that your system was working correctly.

AUDIT PACK READY

DPBI asked you to prove what your AI did six months ago.

What happened: A customer filed a complaint with DPBI. The Board issued a notice asking your company for evidence of how that customer’s data was handled over the previous six months. You had a defined window to respond.

What Pramana did: Generated a sealed, timestamped evidence pack — every AI decision involving that customer, in sequence, with the rule applied and the outcome recorded. The pack was cryptographically verified: nothing had been altered after the fact.

⚖️ Why the law cares: DPBI has the power to request evidence within set timeframes. If you cannot produce it, that itself is a violation. The difference between a resolved complaint and a fine is often whether you can answer: “Show us what your AI decided, and prove it was lawful.”

APPROVED

Your AI sent customer data to a cloud vendor overseas — and that was actually fine.

What happened: Your business uses an overseas cloud vendor to process customer analytics. Your AI routed some customer data to that vendor’s servers in a country not on any government-restricted list.

What Pramana did: Checked the current restricted-country list. The destination was not on it. The transfer proceeded — with a receipt confirming exactly which list was checked, the version of the rule used, and the timestamp of the approval.

⚖️ Why the law cares: Unlike GDPR — which blocks most cross-border transfers by default and requires a country to be declared “adequate” — India’s DPDP Act takes the opposite approach. Transfers are permitted everywhere by default, except to countries the government explicitly restricts. Many Indian businesses assume GDPR rules apply here. They don’t. Pramana checks the actual Indian list — not someone’s memory of European rules.

BLOCKED

Your AI tried to send payment data overseas — DPDP said yes, but another law said no.

What happened: A fintech’s AI routed customer payment records to an overseas data processor. The destination was not on the DPDP restricted-country list. Under DPDP alone, the transfer would have been permitted.

What Pramana did: Checked against both DPDP and applicable sectoral rules. The transfer triggered a payment-sector localization obligation — a separate requirement that payment data must stay within India. The transfer was blocked, and the receipt recorded which specific rule caused the block and why.

⚖️ Why the law cares: DPDP is not the only data law in India. Sector-specific regulators have their own data rules, and where they are stricter than DPDP, the stricter rule applies. A governance system that only checks DPDP will miss these. Pramana governs across the frameworks that apply to your business — not just one.

HUMAN REVIEW

A data breach was detected. Your AI began drafting and sending notifications automatically. Pramana stopped it.

What happened: A security incident was detected that may have exposed customer personal data. Your AI system, detecting the incident, automatically began drafting and scheduling breach notification messages to send to customers.

What Pramana did: Stopped the automated notifications before they went out. Routed them to a human reviewer — because what a company says in a breach notification, to whom, and when, are legal decisions that must be made deliberately by a person with appropriate authority. The reviewer’s approval and the timestamp were sealed in the evidence record.

⚖️ Why the law cares: Under DPDP, a business must notify the Data Protection Board and affected individuals promptly after a breach. What you say in that notification — and what you omit — directly shapes your liability. A notification drafted and sent automatically by AI, with no human review, is not a compliant process. The content, scope, and timing must be a human decision — with a sealed record that it was.

FOR LARGER ORGANISATIONS

Built for the compliance bar that’s coming.

Some organisations — based on the volume, sensitivity, and risk profile of the personal data they process — will be designated as Significant Data Fiduciaries (SDFs) by the government. SDF status brings a heavier layer of obligations on top of the DPDP baseline: an India-based Data Protection Officer, mandatory independent audits, and regular Data Protection Impact Assessments.

Pramana’s evidence ledger is built to produce exactly what an SDF audit requires: a sealed, timestamped record of every governance decision, traceable to the specific statutory provision and policy rule applied at the moment it was made. Your independent auditor or DPO can review the ledger directly — no reconstruction, no manual search.

If your organisation is in banking, healthcare, large-scale e-commerce, or any sector that processes data at scale, SDF designation is not a distant possibility — it is a near-term planning assumption.
